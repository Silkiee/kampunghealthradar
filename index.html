How Kampung Health Radar works

Every week the Communicable Diseases Agency (CDA) publishes a Weekly Infectious Disease Bulletin: dozens of tables and charts written for specialists. This prototype reads those bulletins and answers three everyday questions: What is going around? Is it getting better or worse? What can I do?

Where the numbers come from

  • Dengue: weekly notified cases (dengue fever plus dengue haemorrhagic fever).
  • HFMD and flu & respiratory infections: the average number of people a day seen at polyclinics. Polyclinics see only part of all patients, so treat these as a trend gauge, not a total count.
  • COVID-19: an estimate. We multiply respiratory polyclinic visits by the share of respiratory samples that tested positive for COVID-19.
  • Last year (2025): weeks 1 to 37 come from the "2025" column of each 2026 bulletin. Weeks 38 to 53 were read off the bars in the charts of the latest bulletin. We checked that method against the printed weeks, and it matches to within 1 case.
  • Earlier years (2012 to 2024): weekly dengue cases and respiratory polyclinic visits, plus HFMD polyclinic visits for 2023 and 2024, from CDA's yearly bulletin spreadsheets. Before 2023 those spreadsheets count HFMD differently (notified cases), so HFMD starts in 2023. Respiratory visits in 2020 to 2022 were shaped by COVID-19, and the 2021 and 2022 figures are much higher than in other years.
  • "Usual" means the 5-year median (2021 to 2025) that CDA prints for each week.

How the 3-month outlook is made

The outlook starts from the average of the last three weeks, follows the usual shape of the same months in past years (2012 to 2025 for dengue and respiratory visits, 2023 to 2025 for HFMD), and continues the recent direction, fading it out over time. The darker band is where the number should land about half the time, and the lighter band about 8 times in 10. Both were set by testing the method on every earlier week of 2026. A wide band is a real signal: this year's mid-year dengue surge arrived with little warning. Adding past years through Add data sharpens the seasonal pattern automatically.

"What if we act now?"

The sliders use a simplified rule. Each round of infection shrinks in proportion to how much you cut the spread, after a short delay (about 2 weeks for dengue, while existing mosquitoes and infections run their course). Some cases, such as infections caught overseas, cannot be prevented this way, so we keep 15% of cases untouched. For flu, the action applies only to the share of respiratory illness caused by flu. These are illustrations for planning conversations, not predictions of any specific programme.

Why the map is illustrative

The bulletin reports national totals only. To show what a neighbourhood view could look like, we spread each national number across the 55 URA planning areas by approximate resident population and age profile. HFMD leans towards areas with many young children, and flu and COVID-19 lean towards older estates. For dengue we add a mild weighting for eastern and north-eastern estates and landed-housing areas, where dengue clusters have historically been more common. None of this is real 2026 local data. Switch the map to national figures with the toggle.

Colours compare each area's estimated rate per 100,000 residents with a typical week for Singapore (the 5-year median CDA prints). Moderate is within 20% of typical, High is 20–60% above it, and Very high is more than 60% above. This is why a quiet year for dengue shows mostly pale colours, even at its peak.

Limits

  • Independent prototype. Not affiliated with or endorsed by CDA, MOH or NEA.
  • Bulletin figures are provisional and may be revised.
  • Population figures per planning area are rounded approximations.
  • Advice follows standard public health guidance. It is not medical advice for any individual.

Source: CDA Weekly Infectious Diseases Bulletin 2026. Earlier years: yearly Weekly Infectious Disease Bulletin spreadsheets, 2012 to 2025. Planning area boundaries: URA Master Plan 2019 (data.gov.sg).
